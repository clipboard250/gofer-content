quick checklist, not a lecture

Security website playbook

A simple scroll-through of things security companies (cyber or physical) often miss when they put their site together.

Where sites usually go sideways

first impression

Most visitors decide in a few seconds if they trust the site or not. Busy layouts, random photos, and vague text all push them away before they even read the details.

doing the most

Security as a field is serious. If the site feels all over the place, people assume the service is the same. A calmer layout with visible structure sends a different message.

Bad vs. better patterns

Typical "messy" layout

  • Big hero text that does not say what you actually handle
  • Five different fonts and colors fighting each other
  • Stock photos that do not look like your world
  • Services buried in a long paragraph
  • Contact info hidden in the footer only

More focused layout

  • One clear sentence: who you protect and how
  • Short service list broken into simple chunks
  • Modern visuals that match the type of security you offer
  • Contact button near the top plus phone / form options
  • Enough detail to feel solid, not overloaded

Pieces every security site needs

services

Spell it out clearly. Guards, patrol, monitoring, cyber hardening, cameras, transport, events, consulting — whatever applies. People should not have to guess.

patrol event security cyber defense
contact paths

Make it easy to reach you. A simple contact button, a short form, and a phone number. No hunting through menus.

call request info email
quick read

Keep the intro tight. In the first panel, visitors should learn: what you do, who you serve, and where you operate.

proof

Show evidence without bragging. Licensing, experience, training, tools, or the types of clients you usually help.

visual style

Modern but steady. Dark backgrounds, strong typography, and consistent spacing feel more secure than loud colors and busy shapes.

phones first

Assume they are on mobile. Buttons that are easy to tap, text that does not require zooming, and pages that load fast.

One simple layout that usually works

top of page

Hero line, one short subline, and a clear “Talk to us” or “Request details” button.

middle

Service grid, two or three short proof points, and one real photo if you have it.

bottom

FAQ or a few common questions, plus contact paths repeated so nobody has to scroll back up.

Local view: St. Petersburg and Tampa Bay

coverage zone

Call out the areas you know: St. Petersburg, Pinellas County, downtown corridors, beaches, waterfront venues, and nearby cities like Clearwater or Tampa. That line alone helps visitors feel like you are from here and not just dropping a pin on a map.

Florida realities

Mention hurricane season readiness, nightlife corridors, festivals, and waterfront events. This shows you understand how security shifts when the weather changes or crowds spike.

late-night presence

If you support late hours, say it plainly: late-night patrol, bar and venue closings, overnight monitoring, or patrol around popular trails and parking areas.

ADA essentials

screen readers

Give every meaningful image alt text that describes what is happening, not just file names. Pages should follow a steady heading order so assistive tech can move through the content in a predictable way.

keyboard path

All links and buttons must be reachable by keyboard alone. Focus outlines should stay visible so visitors always know where they are.

brightness

Dark themes still need high contrast. Text should stand out against the background for visitors with limited vision.

SEO notes (AI-friendly)

straight answers

Search systems read for direct statements. Short lines like “We offer event security in St. Petersburg and nearby beaches” are easy for both people and AI tools to surface.

questions people ask

Examples to answer on the site: “What areas in St. Petersburg do you cover?”, “Do you support late-night events?”, “Do you handle waterfront venues?”, “How fast can someone reach you?”.

speed

Fast pages help visitors stay. Smaller image sizes and straightforward code let the site load even when mobile data gets unstable during storms.

Hurricane mode checklist

status banner

During storms, a simple banner at the top can show whether your team is taking calls, on standby, or following county orders.

priorities

Explain what you focus on when weather shifts: protecting sites before landfall, watching for looting after, or checking critical locations when streets reopen.

contact plan

Let clients know the best way to reach you in a storm: direct line, text, or a simple form. Clear steps help when everyone is stressed.

Common questions to answer on the site

who do you protect?

Homes, small businesses, events, warehouses, churches, schools, nightlife spots, or something else. A short line for each helps people self-select.

when are you available?

Daytime only, overnight, weekends, or on-call for special situations. People should not have to guess if their schedule matches yours.

how do you respond?

Briefly explain how you react to alerts: call first, dispatch, patrol, or coordinate with law enforcement when needed.

This is just a reference. Use whatever parts match what you are building and ignore the rest.
